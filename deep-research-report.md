## Core conclusion

The recent literature supports the *direction* of your project, but it also suggests a more careful framing than the draft currently implies. The strongest evidence internationally is that multimodal models can outperform text-only systems **when the task truly depends on cross-modal evidence**, such as out-of-context image reuse, contradictory caption-image pairings, or visually encoded framing. ## What the international propaganda-detection literature already established

The modern computational literature on propaganda detection in news is anchored by the move from **source-level labeling** to **fine-grained content analysis**. Da San Martino et al. (2019) argued that labeling all articles from a propaganda outlet as propaganda creates noisy supervision and weak explainability, and they introduced a fragment-level corpus annotated with 18 propaganda techniques. SemEval-2020 Task 11 then operationalized this line of work at scale, with span identification and technique classification as benchmark subtasks; the organizers report that the strongest systems relied on pretrained transformers and ensembles (Da San Martino et al., 2019, 2020). citeturn19academia1turn18academia0

That literature matters for your project in two ways. First, it validates the **text-only baseline** you propose: XLM-R or a similar multilingual transformer is entirely standard for this task family. XLM-R itself was introduced specifically to improve multilingual masked-language modeling at scale and was shown to outperform mBERT across multiple cross-lingual benchmarks, including low-resource conditions, which makes it a sensible Lithuanian baseline even if you ultimately fine-tune a monolingual or domain-adapted alternative later (Conneau et al., 2019). citeturn40academia0

Second, the textual propaganda literature also supports your interest in interpretable high-level features. Krishnamurthy et al. (2020) showed that sentence-level emotional salience features can improve propaganda-technique detection relative to BERT-only modeling in some settings, while Faye et al. (2024) found that human annotators distinguish pseudo-news from regular news partly through cues tied to vagueness, subjectivity, and style. Hamilton et al. (2024) go further by codifying 22 rhetorical and linguistic features connected to persuasion and scaling their annotation with GPT assistance. This line of work suggests that your proposed manual feature layer is not a detour from the literature; it is arguably where your project can make its clearest cognitive-science contribution, especially if the concepts are operationalized consistently and analyzed independently of final class predictions (Krishnamurthy et al., 2020; Faye et al., 2024; Hamilton et al., 2024). citeturn29academia9turn20academia0turn29academia4

The international literature also shows, however, that propaganda datasets usually do **not** use your exact three-way taxonomy. Most studies classify binary propaganda/non-propaganda, identify techniques, or estimate broader bias or pseudo-news style. That means your “open vs hidden propaganda” distinction is interesting, but it is not directly inherited from an established benchmark. You will have to define it very explicitly, almost as if part of the paper were also a mini-annotation-guideline contribution. A reviewer or examiner will otherwise ask whether “hidden propaganda” is really propaganda, subtle bias, or merely persuasive framing. citeturn19academia1turn18academia0turn20academia0turn38academia3

## What multimodal research says about hidden propaganda and visual framing

The strongest international support for your multimodal hypothesis comes from research on **out-of-context misinformation**, **image–text inconsistency**, and **visual framing**. SAFE showed early that explicitly modeling similarity and mismatch between text and images improves fake-news detection relative to treating each modality independently. NewsCLIPpings then created a challenging benchmark in which the text and image are individually real but misleading when paired together, precisely the kind of situation that resembles hidden manipulation rather than overt fabrication. COSMOS similarly targeted out-of-context misuse of authentic images and reported strong performance from models that ground textual claims in visual evidence rather than relying on language alone (SAFE authors, 2020; Luo et al., 2021; Aneja et al., 2021). citeturn25academia0turn14academia0turn27academia3

This is directly relevant to your idea of hidden propaganda. A great deal of modern manipulation is not a falsified photo or an openly propagandistic slogan. It is the quieter move of choosing *this* image, *this* crop, *this* person, *this* symbol, or *this* emotional visual setting around otherwise plausible text. That is why multimodality can matter more for hidden propaganda than for open propaganda: the text may remain deniable while the image does the persuasive work. Newer work in computational framing points in the same direction. Arora et al. (2025) argue that automated frame analysis of political communication has been overly text-centric and that news photographs carry latent meaning that changes how topics are framed. Earlier visual-bias work likewise showed that image content can encode political leaning, even when modeled separately from the associated text (Arora et al., 2025; Thomas & Kovashka, 2019). citeturn38academia3turn24academia2

That said, the multimodal literature also contains an important warning. VERITE was introduced precisely because widely used multimodal misinformation benchmarks can be contaminated by **unimodal bias**, allowing “multimodal” progress that is not truly multimodal. Papadopoulos et al. (2024) push the critique further by showing that simple similarity-based baselines can match or beat more elaborate systems on NewsCLIPpings and VERITE, raising doubts about whether some models are reasoning about factuality at all. This is one of the most important findings for your project: if you want to claim that images expose hidden propaganda, you need to rule out the possibility that the model is merely learning source aesthetics, topic distribution, image quality, or recurring outlet-specific templates (Papadopoulos et al., 2023, 2024). citeturn27academia1turn27academia0

Several other studies further support the idea that visuals contain ideological or persuasive traces. Chen et al. (2021) found systematic visual differences between conspiracy and counter-conspiracy videos, including lower color variance and brightness in conspiracy thumbnails and early video segments. Although that study concerns conspiracy videos rather than Lithuanian news, it supports your core theoretical claim that manipulative communication can leak through *visual style* even when the text is less explicit. The implication for your project is that “hidden propaganda” may be captured not only by object-level concepts such as flags, soldiers, and public figures, but also by image-level cues such as saturation, darkness, crowding, compositional aggressiveness, and emotional intensity (Chen et al., 2021). citeturn24academia1

Finally, CLIP remains a defensible engineering choice for this setup. Radford et al. (2021) introduced CLIP as a contrastive image–text model trained on large-scale image-caption pairs, and that design has become one of the standard foundations for image–text alignment and fusion. In practical terms, this means your proposal to extract text embeddings from XLM-R and image embeddings from CLIP or a CLIP-like encoder is perfectly consistent with the international literature—provided you evaluate whether the fusion really adds value and does not just amplify dataset shortcuts (Radford et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2022). citeturn40academia1turn39academia0

## LLM-assisted annotation and interpretability

The literature is broadly favorable to **LLM-assisted annotation**, but not to uncritical **LLM-only annotation**, especially in low-resource settings. Hamilton et al. (2024) provide one of the most directly relevant papers for your design: they created an interpretable propaganda-feature annotation workflow where a small human-annotated subset was used to fine-tune GPT-3.5 to annotate the remaining data, achieving performance comparable to GPT-4 at much lower cost. Raza et al. (2024) similarly show that GPT-4-assisted labels verified by human experts can improve fake-news classification relative to weaker distant supervision, while still finding that BERT-like encoder models remain stronger classifiers than LLMs in the final detection task. These results strongly support your plan to use an agentic labeling system for data expansion while still training conventional classifiers for the final benchmark (Hamilton et al., 2024; Raza et al., 2024). citeturn29academia4turn37academia3

The caution comes from the low-resource and validation literature. Jadhav et al. (2024) show that LLMs used as annotators degrade substantially in a low-resource-language setting, while Calderon et al. (2025) argue that replacing human annotators requires a formal statistical justification procedure rather than intuition. A more propaganda-specific hybrid-annotation study by Sahitaj et al. (2025) also indicates that LLM pre-annotation can improve annotation speed and agreement when humans remain in the loop. Taken together, these works imply that your custom Lithuanian dataset should **not** rely on raw LLM labels as ground truth. The safer route is: use the LLM system for pre-labeling, manually verify a substantial subset, compute agreement, and reserve a fully human-labeled test set that the model never sees during weak-label generation (Jadhav et al., 2024; Calderon et al., 2025; Sahitaj et al., 2025). citeturn21academia1turn21academia0turn37academia2

Interpretability is where your project can become stronger than many standard student-classification papers. The recent explainability literature suggests that generic post-hoc tools such as SHAP or LIME are useful but incomplete for multimodal reasoning. Lourenço and Paes (2022) propose modality-level explanations, SNIFFER uses a multimodal LLM to generate evidence-based explanations for out-of-context misinformation, and Zhang et al. (2023) use neural-symbolic decomposition to identify cross-modal contradictions while preserving competitive accuracy. Meanwhile, work on multimodal concept bottleneck models shows that if you care about concepts like *military presence*, *national symbol*, or *known public figure*, it is often better to model them explicitly than to hope that a black-box explanation method will recover them faithfully afterward (Lourenço & Paes, 2022; Zhang et al., 2023; Qi et al., 2024; Srivastava et al., 2024; Selvaraj et al., 2024). citeturn16academia2turn27academia2turn16academia4turn30academia0turn30academia3

A practical consequence follows from that. If your secondary goal is to identify “cognitive triggers” separating open from hidden propaganda, then a pure late-stage SHAP analysis on fused embeddings is probably **not enough**. A stronger design would use your high-level concepts as either auxiliary labels, a concept bottleneck, or a multi-task prediction layer. That would let you report both class performance and concept-level behavior in a way that is more interpretable and better aligned with the cognitive-science framing of the exam. citeturn30academia0turn30academia3turn39academia2

## What the literature implies for your exact project design

Overall, the literature supports your first hypothesis in a **qualified** form: a multimodal model is most likely to beat a text-only model **if** your dataset contains enough cases where the visuals carry unique persuasive information or cross-modal inconsistency. If hidden propaganda in your corpus mainly consists of subtle lexical framing with generic stock photos, the multimodal advantage may be small. If, however, hidden propaganda routinely relies on symbolic imagery, emotionally charged framing, selective scene choice, or image–text tension, then your hypothesis becomes much stronger. The existing out-of-context and framing literature gives you a theoretical basis for expecting such gains, especially for the hidden class (Luo et al., 2021; Aneja et al., 2021; Papadopoulos et al., 2023; Arora et al., 2025). citeturn14academia0turn27academia3turn27academia1turn38academia3

Your second hypothesis is also credible, but only if the concepts are carefully defined and reliably annotated. The international literature suggests several candidate feature families that would be defensible in a Lithuanian news setting: emotional salience; vagueness and subjectivity; explicit national or military symbols; prominent politicians or public figures; crowd size and confrontation cues; image brightness or saturation; and cross-modal entity consistency. These are not random engineering choices—they are all supported by nearby propaganda, framing, or misinformation research (Krishnamurthy et al., 2020; Faye et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2021; Fu et al., 2024). citeturn29academia9turn20academia0turn24academia1turn16academia0

The literature also points to four concrete design improvements for your project. First, define **open propaganda** and **hidden propaganda** in an annotation manual with examples and decision rules; otherwise, inter-annotator agreement may collapse. Second, create **hard negatives** and modality-balanced splits so that the multimodal model must reason across modalities rather than memorize outlets, topics, or image styles. Third, use **LLM pre-annotation plus human verification**, not LLM-only labels. Fourth, consider making your concept layer structurally explicit rather than purely post-hoc. These changes would move the project from a promising idea to a literature-aligned experiment. citeturn27academia1turn27academia0turn21academia0turn21academia1turn29academia4turn37academia2turn30academia0

There is also a very practical sourcing lesson from the literature. Because Lithuanian multimodal propaganda datasets appear sparse, you may want to treat your data pipeline as a combination of: verified Lithuanian mainstream outlets; identified pro-Kremlin or manipulative outlets; archived material when live pages disappear; and multilingual transfer resources such as EUvsDisinfo as auxiliary pretraining or comparative material rather than direct substitutes. EUvsDisinfo is especially relevant because it is a multilingual dataset of pro-Kremlin disinformation articles and trustworthy counterparts, collected from expert-written debunks, which makes it closer to your intended domain than generic fake-news corpora (Leite et al., 2024). citeturn3academia4turn9academia7
