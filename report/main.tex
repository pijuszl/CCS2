\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Automated propaganda detection in Lithuanian news has so far relied on
text-only models and binary labels.
This paper addresses two gaps simultaneously: it introduces a three-class
taxonomy --- \emph{non-propaganda}, \emph{open} (overt), and \emph{hidden}
(covert) --- and brings the visual modality into the picture.
The cognitive-science motivation is that hidden propaganda operates through
emotional visual framing that bypasses deliberate (System-2) evaluation
\parencite{kahneman2011thinking}, making images diagnostic precisely where
text is deliberately ambiguous.
We construct a corpus of 3,647 labelled Lithuanian news articles (2,102
image-bearing) by mapping three existing corpora (HALT-PROP, LITUND, DIGIRES)
to the new taxonomy and annotating a newly scraped propagandasource dataset
using a large-language-model pipeline (Claude~3.5 cross-validated against
Qwen3-80B).
A late-fusion multimodal classifier combining XLM-RoBERTa text embeddings with
CLIP ViT-B/32 image features is evaluated against a text-only baseline in a
$2\times2$ design (modality $\times$ data augmentation).
Our $\chi^2$ feature analysis yields significant discriminators already:
\emph{country/leader mentions} ($\chi^2{=}415.3$, $p{<}0.001$),
\emph{quotation balance} ($\chi^2{=}73.0$, $p{<}0.001$), and
\emph{us-vs-them framing} ($\chi^2{=}36.8$, $p{<}0.001$) cleanly separate the
three classes, grounding the open/hidden distinction empirically.
Deep-learning model evaluation is in progress; the complete quantitative
results will be appended to the project repository upon completion.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ================================================================

The Baltic information environment has been a sustained target of
influence operations, particularly after Russia's 2014 annexation of Crimea
\parencite{vasnetsov2026dualinput}.
Lithuanian-language websites aligned with Kremlin messaging regularly publish
content that blends factual surface features with selective framing, emotional
imagery, and implicit narrative construction.
Detecting such manipulation at scale is a prerequisite for effective media
literacy interventions.

Existing Lithuanian propaganda detection systems are text-only and treat the
task as binary (propaganda / non-propaganda)
\parencite{haltprop2025,vasnetsov2026dualinput}.
This framing misses two things.
First, it ignores the visual channel, even though images are often the primary
vector for emotional framing \parencite{entman1993framing}.
Second, it collapses the functionally important distinction between
\emph{open} propaganda (explicit loaded language, slogans, name-calling ---
manipulation that a moderately attentive reader can recognise) and
\emph{hidden} propaganda (selective framing, false balance, whataboutism,
implicit narrative --- manipulation designed to evade deliberate scrutiny).

The cognitive-science framework that motivates this distinction is dual-process
theory \parencite{kahneman2011thinking}.
System~1 processing is fast, automatic, and associative; it responds to
emotional imagery and narrative tone before System~2 (slow, deliberate,
analytical) engages with propositional content.
Hidden propaganda is engineered to operate at the System~1 level: by the time
a reader's deliberate attention reaches the claims in the text, the emotional
framing delivered by images, headlines, and structural choices has already
shaped interpretation.
A classifier that can access the visual modality should therefore gain an
advantage specifically on the hidden class --- the prediction that drives
Hypothesis~1 below.

\paragraph{Research questions and hypotheses.}
\textbf{RQ1} (H1): Does a text+image multimodal model outperform a text-only
baseline at three-class classification, with the largest gain on the
\emph{hidden} class?\\
\textbf{RQ2} (H2): Does augmenting training with LLM-labelled scraped data
improve classification performance?\\
\textbf{RQ3} (H3): Which interpretable linguistic and visual features most
reliably separate the three classes, and do they correspond to theoretically
predicted framing cues?

This paper makes the following contributions: (1) a three-class Lithuanian
propaganda corpus with 3,647 labelled articles; (2) a principled label mapping
from technique-annotated existing corpora to the overt/covert taxonomy;
(3) an LLM-in-the-loop annotation pipeline with cross-model validation;
(4) a late-fusion multimodal architecture; and (5) empirical $\chi^2$ evidence
for the open/hidden feature boundary.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ================================================================

\subsection{Multimodal machine learning}

Integrating text and image representations has become a central theme in
machine learning, with applications ranging from visual question answering to
cross-modal retrieval \parencite{liang2023multimodal}.
For news analysis specifically, image-text consistency is a strong indicator
of fabrication \parencite{liang2023multimodal}.
\textit{Late fusion} --- encoding each modality independently and merging at
the decision layer --- offers flexibility when images are sometimes absent and
is theoretically justified when the modalities carry complementary rather
than redundant information, as we expect for propaganda framing
\parencite{liang2023multimodal}.
Multilingual joint models such as jina-clip-v2 \parencite{jinaclip2024} embed
long text and high-resolution images in a shared space across 89 languages
(including Lithuanian), making them a natural alternative baseline.

\subsection{Propaganda detection}

Fine-grained propaganda analysis has advanced from binary classifiers to
multi-label technique detection across 18 persuasion techniques
\parencite{dasanmartino2019proppy}.
These systems are primarily English-language and do not capture the
overt/covert dimension.
For Lithuanian, the HALT-PROP corpus \parencite{haltprop2025} provides
the largest labelled resource with technique annotations, and
\textcite{vasnetsov2026dualinput} demonstrate strong binary classification
performance using a dual-input (headline + body) neural network.
Our work builds directly on HALT-PROP and the EPJ system as the closest
text-only baseline, extending it with the visual modality and the three-class
taxonomy.

\subsection{Framing theory and cognitive foundations}

\textcite{entman1993framing} defines framing as the selective presentation of
information to promote a particular interpretation.
Crucially, framing operates partly below deliberate awareness: the placement of
an emotionally charged photograph, the choice of which leader is depicted as
the adversary, and the structural emphasis given to certain voices all shape
interpretation before explicit argumentation is processed.
\textcite{kahneman2011thinking} provides the cognitive substrate: System~1
primes an emotional and narrative context that System~2 subsequently rationalises
rather than questions.
This predicts that hidden propaganda --- which deliberately keeps its
propositional content ambiguous --- relies more heavily on visual framing than
open propaganda, and that a classifier with image access will close this gap.
The prediction is operationalised in H1 (largest multimodal gain on the
\emph{hidden} class).

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
%% ================================================================

\subsection{Existing corpora}

We combine three existing Lithuanian corpora into a unified training resource.

\textbf{HALT-PROP} \parencite{haltprop2025} contains 2,870 articles with binary
propaganda labels; a 1,000-article subset carries fine-grained technique
annotations across 14 categories and six geopolitical narrative labels.
This is the backbone of our labelled training data.

\textbf{LITUND} is a fact-checker-sourced corpus of 294 unreliable Lithuanian
news articles.
Its articles are shorter on average and cover a wider range of topics than
HALT-PROP; they provide diversity in the open/hidden split.

\textbf{DIGIRES} is a CLARIN-LT COVID-19 disinformation corpus of 351 articles.
Its narrow thematic scope is a limitation but adds domain variety that may help
prevent overfitting to specific political frames.

\subsection{Label mapping to the three-class taxonomy}
\label{sec:mapping}

The corpora use heterogeneous labels.
We map them to \{non, open, hidden\} as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{non}: articles with a non-propaganda binary label.
  \item \textbf{open}: articles with at least one \emph{overt} technique tag
        (name-calling, loaded language, slogans, explicit appeal to emotion or
        prejudice, repetition, flag-waving).
  \item \textbf{hidden}: articles with at least one \emph{subtle} technique tag
        (selective framing, whataboutism, appeal to authority, vagueness,
        false balance, implicit narrative construction) but \emph{no} overt tags.
  \item Articles with a propaganda label but \emph{no} technique annotation
        (approximately 1,730 rows) cannot be placed in the taxonomy and are
        excluded from supervised training.
        They retain their binary label for potential future use.
\end{itemize}

This mapping is a methodological choice and a validity threat: it assumes the
technique taxonomy cleanly partitions overt from covert manipulation.
We retain the original \texttt{native\_label} for traceability.

\subsection{Scraped corpus and image collection}

To introduce the visual modality, we scraped text, metadata, and main article
images from two sources identified as pro-Kremlin outlets:
\textit{rubaltic.lt} (437 articles; Sputnik Lithuania affiliate) and
\textit{bukimevieningi.lt} (1,426 articles; pro-Kremlin commentary).
Neutral sources (LRT, lrytas.lt, Delfi) were identified but encountered access
restrictions; adding them is a priority for future work and would remove the
source-identity confound described in \S\ref{sec:discuss}.
All scraping respected \texttt{robots.txt}, applied per-domain rate limits,
and cached HTML to avoid repeat requests.

Scraped images inherit a \emph{binary} weak label (propaganda / non) from
their source's \texttt{source\_type} field.
These binary labels are used only for an auxiliary vision-encoder pre-training
head (\S\ref{sec:arch}) and are never used as three-class gold labels.

\subsection{LLM annotation pipeline}

Scraped articles require three-class annotation.
Each article is passed to Claude~3.5 Sonnet via a structured batch prompt
containing: a system-role declaration, the annotation rubric (three worked
examples per class), and an explicit JSON output schema
\{\texttt{label}, \texttt{confidence}, \texttt{rationale}, \texttt{features}\}.
Articles below a confidence threshold of 0.6 are flagged for human review.
For cross-validation, the same articles are independently labelled by
Qwen3-80B-A3B-Thinking (4-bit int4 quantisation, A100 GPU);
agreement is assessed via Cohen's~$\kappa$ and gates downstream use of labels
at $\kappa \geq 0.70$.
All prompts are version-controlled under \texttt{src/annotation/prompts/}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
%% ================================================================

\subsection{Text baseline: XLM-RoBERTa}

Our text-only baseline fine-tunes \texttt{xlm-roberta-base}
\parencite{conneau2020xlmr}, a 12-layer multilingual transformer pre-trained
on 2.5\,TB of text in 100 languages (including Lithuanian).
The \texttt{[CLS]} token representation is passed through dropout ($p{=}0.1$)
and a linear head producing three logits.
Maximum sequence length is 512 SentencePiece tokens; longer articles are
truncated.

\subsection{Vision encoder: CLIP ViT-B/32}
\label{sec:vision}

We use the image tower of CLIP ViT-B/32 \parencite{radford2021clip}
as the vision encoder.
CLIP's visual backbone maps $224{\times}224$ images to 512-dimensional
normalised embeddings.
Only the image tower is used: CLIP's text tower is English-only with a
77-token limit and cannot process Lithuanian.
Early backbone layers are frozen; the top transformer blocks and linear
projection are fine-tuned.

\subsection{Late-fusion multimodal architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

The primary model concatenates text and image embeddings into a joint
representation and passes it through a shallow fusion head:

\[
\mathbf{z} = \mathrm{LayerNorm}([\,\mathbf{h}_\text{text} \,\|\, \mathbf{h}_\text{img}\,])
   \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{1280}
\]
\[
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = W_2\,\mathrm{GELU}\!\left(\mathrm{Dropout}(W_1 \mathbf{z})\right),
   \quad W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{512\times1280},\; W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times512}
\]

\emph{Modality dropout} (probability 0.1) zeros the image branch stochastically
during training, encouraging robustness when images are absent at inference.

An \emph{auxiliary binary head} on the vision encoder is trained simultaneously
on the scraped weak image labels (propaganda / non), acting as a form of
multi-task pre-fine-tuning that shapes image representations toward
propaganda-relevant content without requiring three-class ground truth for every
image.

\subsection{Experimental design}

We run a $2{\times}2$ factorial:
\textbf{modality} (text-only XLM-R vs.\ late-fusion multimodal)
$\times$
\textbf{data condition} (existing corpora only vs.\ existing + LLM-labelled scraped corpus).
The H1 comparison is performed on the image-bearing subset to ensure the
modality contrast is not confounded by dataset size.
Additional ablations: vision-only (sanity baseline),
jina-clip-v2 joint model \parencite{jinaclip2024},
modality-dropout robustness,
unweighted cross-entropy (no focal loss), and
multimodal without auxiliary weak-label head.

\subsection{Training setup}

All models are optimised with AdamW (lr $= 2{\times}10^{-5}$,
weight decay $10^{-2}$), focal loss ($\gamma{=}2$)
\parencite{lin2017focal} with per-class weights, gradient accumulation
(effective batch size 32), mixed-precision (fp16), and early stopping on
validation macro-F1 (patience 2, up to 4 epochs).
All runs use seed 42, log the git hash and configuration, and execute on an
NVIDIA A100 GPU.

\subsection{High-level feature extraction}

Fourteen interpretable features are computed per article for the $\chi^2$
and SHAP analyses (H3 / RQ3):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Textual (6)}: loaded-lexicon density, country/leader entity
        mentions (NER), us-vs-them pronoun ratio, uncertainty/hedging markers,
        quotation source balance, sensational headline score.
  \item \textbf{Visual (8)}: national symbol, military presence, known public
        figure, crowd/protest, map/territory, religious symbol, emotional
        facial expression, image emotional valence --- each extracted via
        zero-shot CLIP prompt similarity.
\end{itemize}

$\chi^2$ tests with Holm correction assess feature--class association.
SHAP values \parencite{lundberg2017shap} from a gradient-boosted classifier
fitted on the feature table quantify relative importance and interaction
effects.

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
Primary: macro-F1 (weights all classes equally, robust to imbalance).
Secondary: per-class precision/recall/F1, confusion matrices.
Significance: McNemar's paired test for two classifiers on the same test set;
non-parametric bootstrap CIs ($B{=}1{,}000$) on macro-F1.

\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Annotation pipeline output}

The Claude~3.5 pipeline processed all 437 rubaltic.lt articles,
producing labels with a mean confidence of 0.89 (sd 0.04).
The distribution --- 368 \emph{open} (84.2\%) and 69 \emph{hidden} (15.8\%) ---
is consistent with rubaltic.lt's predominantly explicit rhetorical register
(heavily loaded headlines, direct anti-Western framing).
The lower proportion of hidden articles reflects the outlet's style rather than
a limitation of the classifier; bukimevieningi.lt, which publishes more
analytical commentary, is expected to show a higher hidden fraction.

Cross-model validation ($\kappa$ between Claude and Qwen3-80B) is currently in
progress on the A100 GPU; the $\kappa$ report will be appended to the project
repository at \url{https://github.com/pijuszl/CCS2}.
A formal $\kappa$ threshold of 0.70 gates use of the scraped labels in the
training experiments.

\textbf{Country/leader mentions} emerges as the dominant discriminator by a
wide margin ($\chi^2 = 415.3$, more than five times the next feature).
This is consistent with H3: open propaganda explicitly mobilises named political
actors as adversaries, whereas non-propaganda reporting contextualises such
references and hidden propaganda avoids explicit attribution.

\textbf{Quotation balance} ($\chi^2 = 73.0$) is the theoretically most
interesting finding.
The hidden class shows the most skewed quotation sourcing, supporting the
hypothesis that covert framing operates through structural voice allocation
rather than explicit claims: hidden-propaganda articles present superficially
multi-sourced text where one perspective systematically receives more and
more-prominent quotation.

\textbf{Us-vs-them ratio} ($\chi^2 = 36.8$) reflects in-group/out-group
pronoun distribution (\emph{we}/\emph{our} vs.\ \emph{they}/\emph{their}).
Both propaganda classes differ from non-propaganda, but in different directions:
open propaganda shows the highest ratio (direct othering), while hidden
propaganda shows a more moderate ratio consistent with constructing an implicit
out-group through narrative choice rather than direct address.

\textbf{Uncertainty markers} ($\chi^2 = 14.4$) being significant supports the
observation that hidden propaganda hedges its claims to maintain deniability,
while open propaganda states positions directly.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discuss}
%% ================================================================

\subsection{Empirical grounding of the open/hidden taxonomy}

The $\chi^2$ results demonstrate that the three-class taxonomy is not merely
a theoretical convenience --- it is empirically separable using relatively
simple linguistic features.
Four features, each derived from explicit theoretical predictions about
propaganda technique, achieve significance after multiplicity correction on
a corpus of 3,647 articles.
This is notable because it does not depend on deep learning: the taxonomy can
be at least partially recovered from surface-level text features alone,
validating the annotation rubric and the label-mapping procedure.

The magnitude of the country/leader-mentions effect ($\chi^2 = 415.3$) is
striking.
It suggests that one of the clearest markers separating open propaganda from
both non-propaganda and hidden propaganda is simply whether political adversaries
are named explicitly.
From a framing-theory perspective \parencite{entman1993framing}, this
corresponds to the \emph{salience} dimension: open propaganda makes certain
actors maximally salient by naming them; hidden propaganda achieves the same
attitudinal effect through structural means (what is omitted, which voices are
quoted, where they appear in the article).

\subsection{Expectations for the multimodal experiments}

The dual-process prediction (images bypass System-2 scrutiny and are therefore
more diagnostic for hidden propaganda) would be confirmed if the per-class
F1 gain from adding the image modality is largest on \emph{hidden}.
If instead the gain is largest on \emph{open}, this would suggest that images
in this corpus primarily reinforce overt rhetorical choices (e.g., inflammatory
photographs accompanying explicitly loaded text) rather than substituting for
subtle textual cues.
Either outcome has theoretical interest.

\subsection{Limitations and validity threats}

\textbf{Source-identity confound.}
Both scraped sources are propaganda outlets; no neutral-source scraped images
are currently available.
The vision encoder thus sees only propaganda images during training on the
scraped set, making it difficult to disentangle propaganda-related visual
content from outlet-specific visual style (layout, logo placement, stock-photo
preferences).
This is mitigated by evaluating on the existing-corpus test set (which
contains no scraped data) but remains a confound for the scraped-condition runs.

\textbf{Label uncertainty.}
The technique-to-taxonomy mapping (Section~\ref{sec:mapping}) treats
technique categories as mutually exclusive; in practice, an article may employ
both overt and covert techniques.
Articles with mixed technique profiles were assigned to the dominant class,
introducing label noise at the open/hidden boundary.

\textbf{LLM annotation pending validation.}
Claude's 437 scraped labels have not yet been validated against a human gold
standard.
Qwen cross-validation is in progress; if $\kappa < 0.70$, the scraped labels
will be treated as noisy supervision rather than gold labels, and the
+scraped condition will be reported with the caveat.

\textbf{Truncation.}
Lithuanian propaganda articles are often lengthy; the 512-token XLM-R limit
discards approximately 30\% of the text in the longest quartile of articles.
Sliding-window pooling is a natural extension.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ================================================================

This paper has presented a three-class Lithuanian propaganda corpus,
a technique-to-taxonomy label mapping, a multimodal scraping and annotation
pipeline, and a late-fusion XLM-RoBERTa + CLIP architecture for
overt/covert propaganda detection.

The $\chi^2$ feature analysis provides the first empirical quantification of
the linguistic features that separate open from hidden Lithuanian propaganda,
confirming that the open/hidden taxonomy is grounded in detectable surface-level
signals.
Country/leader mentions and quotation balance emerge as the strongest
discriminators, consistent with framing-theory predictions.

Model training is in progress; we predict that the visual modality will
provide the largest precision gain on the \emph{hidden} class, consistent with
the dual-process hypothesis.
Full numerical results will be available in the project repository by 4 June
2026.

\paragraph{Future work} should address three priorities:
(1) scraping neutral-source (LRT, Delfi) images to balance the visual training
data and remove the source-identity confound;
(2) recruiting human annotators for a formal inter-annotator reliability study;
(3) extending the taxonomy and pipeline to Latvian and Estonian, where
analogous influence operations are documented.

\paragraph{Ethics.}
Data collection respected \texttt{robots.txt} and applied rate limiting.
Images may depict identifiable persons and are stored solely for academic
analysis under the principle of minimal data collection.
Classification outputs are article-level decisions under a documented rubric
and must not be used for automated censorship.
